\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Diffusion and flow models are widely used for high-fidelity image generation~\citep{ho2020ddpm,song2021sde,dhariwal2021diffusion,lipman2022flow}. Recent Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architectures combine diffusion modeling with transformer backbones and show gains as model size increases~\citep{peebles2023scalable,ma2024sit}. Despite these advances, training diffusion transformers remains computationally intensive, motivating growing interest in methods that improve representation learning to accelerate optimization and enhance generative performance~\citep{yu2024repa,jiang2025sra,haghighi2026layersync,wang2025dispersive}.

Representation alignment (RA) has been explored along two complementary directions. External methods transfer semantic structure from pretrained representation encoders such as DINOv2 or JEPA-style models~\citep{yu2024repa,oquab2023dinov2,chen2024jepa}. Internal methods derive supervision from representations within the diffusion transformer itself~\citep{jiang2025sra,haghighi2026layersync}. We focus on the internal setting, where source and target activations come from different network depths.

Internal alignment is challenging because activations at different depths carry different semantic information. Shallow layers primarily capture local patterns and low-level structure, whereas deeper layers encode more abstract concepts~\citep{mukhopadhyay2024,kim2025,stracke2025,raghu2021visiontransformers,gromov2024unreasonable}. Consequently, directly connecting these layers without manual tuning often yields suboptimal results, leading existing internal approaches to rely on aligning carefully selected layerpairs~\citep{jiang2025sra,haghighi2026layersync}. A fixed-pair objective supervises only one cross-depth relation and does not explicitly model how that relation varies elsewhere in the hierarchy.
 
Our transition diagnostic shows that prediction error varies significantly with both layer separation and source position (\Cref{fig:projection_layout_observation}(b,c)). Consequently, relying on a single heuristically chosen matching pair fails to fully exploit the diverse and beneficial representations distributed across other layers, a limitation also evident in LayerSync [cite LayerSync] (see Table X). Internal representation alignment thus raises a crucial question beyond merely selecting which layers to align: how do these representations transform throughout the network hierarchy? To capitalize on this rich information, we propose learning an amortized, layer-aware conditional network that models these transitions to improve alignment.

We instantiate this idea with \emph{LARA}, an amortized predictor shared across source--target layer pairs. Given a source representation, LARA predicts its corresponding target-layer representation, conditioned on the source layer, target layer, and their gap. Sharing the predictor across pairs captures common cross-layer transformations while adapting to each transition.
Prior methods still do not fully use information from different layers and can struggle to model transitions between them. To address these issues, we introduce two complementary objectives: compositional consistency and cross-layer redundancy reduction. Compositional consistency encourages long-range predictions to agree with compositions of shorter transitions using a slowly updated target predictor, while cross-layer redundancy reduction limits excessive similarity between layer representations.

Our study makes three contributions. First, we formulate internal representation alignment as learning a shared family of depth-conditioned transitions instead of matching one fixed pair of hidden states. Second, we introduce LARA's layer-conditioned predictor, compositional consistency objective, and cross-layer redundancy regularizer as a joint training framework. Third, controlled experiments clarify the role of each part of our layer-aware framework, including explicit depth conditioning and multi-pair supervision. We further show that LARA generalizes across latent-space and pixel-space image generation, as well as text-conditioned motion generation, using SiT at multiple scales, DiT, and JiT.
