\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Diffusion and flow models are widely used for high-fidelity image generation~\citep{ho2020ddpm,song2021sde,dhariwal2021diffusion,lipman2022flow}. Recent Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architectures combine diffusion modeling with transformer backbones and show gains as model size increases~\citep{peebles2023scalable,ma2024sit}. Despite these advances, training diffusion transformers remains computationally intensive, motivating growing interest in methods that improve representation learning to accelerate optimization and enhance generative performance~\citep{yu2024repa,jiang2025sra,haghighi2026layersync,wang2025dispersive}.

Representation alignment (RA) has been explored along two complementary directions. External methods transfer semantic structure from pretrained representation encoders such as DINOv2 or JEPA-style models~\citep{yu2024repa,oquab2023dinov2,chen2024jepa}. Internal methods derive supervision from representations within the diffusion transformer itself~\citep{jiang2025sra,haghighi2026layersync}. We focus on the internal setting, where source and target activations come from different network depths.

Internal alignment is challenging because activations at different depths carry different semantic information. Shallow layers primarily capture local patterns and low-level structure, whereas deeper layers encode more abstract concepts~\citep{mukhopadhyay2024,kim2025,stracke2025,raghu2021visiontransformers,gromov2024unreasonable}. Consequently, directly connecting these layers without manual tuning often yields suboptimal results, leading existing internal approaches to rely on aligning carefully selected layer pairs~\citep{jiang2025sra,haghighi2026layersync}. A fixed-pair objective supervises only one cross-depth relation, leaving relations between other layer pairs unused as alignment signals.
 
Pair-selection ablations in prior work show that useful guidance extends beyond a single best-performing pair. In LayerSync's SiT-XL/2 ablation at $100\mathrm{K}$ iterations, aligning blocks $(6,18)$ yields an FID of $15.39$, while other pairs such as $(9,17)$ and $(9,22)$ also improve over the unaligned baseline ($16.06$ and $17.82$ versus $26.53$; Table 11 of~\citep{haghighi2026layersync}). SRA reports a similar pattern on SiT-B/2 at $400\mathrm{K}$ iterations: $(3,8)$ yields an FID of $29.10$, while $(4,8)$ and $(4,10)$ achieve $30.00$ and $30.65$, respectively, compared with $33.02$ for the baseline (Table 1 of~\citep{jiang2025sra}). These pair-dependent gains suggest that useful cross-depth guidance is distributed across multiple relations. This motivates learning how representations transform across the hierarchy so that alignment can draw on more than one selected pair.

To model these pair-dependent relations jointly, we introduce \emph{LARA}, a multi-pair alignment framework built around a shared, layer-aware transition predictor and two complementary objectives. Directly aligning hidden states across many depths is difficult because their statistics and semantic content vary throughout the hierarchy. LARA addresses this mismatch by predicting each target-layer representation from a source state, with the mapping conditioned on the source and target depths. Extending prediction across many layer pairs introduces two further challenges. First, broad cross-depth predictability can make hidden states overly similar, so a cross-layer redundancy loss encourages layer-specific structure. Second, long-range transitions are harder to learn directly; decomposing them into shorter transitions provides an easier intermediate learning path. We therefore introduce a compositional consistency loss that encourages a direct long-range prediction to agree with the composition of shorter transitions.

Our study makes three contributions. First, we formulate internal representation alignment as learning a shared family of depth-conditioned transitions instead of matching one fixed pair of hidden states. Second, we introduce LARA's layer-conditioned predictor, compositional consistency objective, and cross-layer redundancy regularizer as a joint training framework. Third, controlled experiments clarify the role of each part of our layer-aware framework, including explicit depth conditioning and multi-pair supervision. We further show that LARA generalizes across latent-space and pixel-space image generation, as well as text-conditioned motion generation, using SiT at multiple scales, DiT, and JiT.
