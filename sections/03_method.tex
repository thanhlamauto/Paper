\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Preliminary and Empirical Motivation}

We build on the stochastic-interpolant formulation used by SiT~\citep{ma2024sit}. Given a data latent $x_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$, Gaussian noise $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and timestep $t\in[0,1]$, the interpolation path is
\begin{equation}
x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon ,
\label{eq:stochastic_interpolant}
\end{equation}
with the target velocity
\[
u_t=\dot{\alpha}_t x_0+\dot{\sigma}_t\epsilon .
\]
A diffusion transformer $v_\theta$ predicts this velocity from $(x_t,t,c)$ by minimizing
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon,t,c}
\left[
\left\|v_\theta(x_t,t,c)-u_t\right\|_2^2
\right].
\label{eq:flow_matching_loss}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Layer-wise activation analysis.}
Following PCA-based representation visualizations used in recent alignment work~\citep{jiang2025sra,yu2024repa,singh2025irepa}, we project SiT-XL/2 hidden tokens onto their leading three principal components in \Cref{fig:projection_layout_observation}(a). The maps show a consistent spatial scaffold across depth and denoising time, while the semantic emphasis changes across blocks. This suggests that layers share reusable structure but are not interchangeable. Direct matching of one hand-picked pair, as in LayerSync~\citep{haghighi2026layersync}, or supervision from a narrow internal EMA teacher, as in SRA~\citep{jiang2025sra}, can exploit part of this structure but ties the signal to a limited alignment relation. LARA instead learns a shared transition interface: common structure is captured by shared parameters, while source, target, and timestep embeddings adapt the mapping to each pair.

\paragraph{Transition difficulty across depth}
\Cref{fig:projection_layout_observation}(b,c) quantifies how prediction error varies across the representation hierarchy. It rises from 0.025 in the shortest-gap quartile to 0.152 in the longest. Source position matters as well: the middle two quartiles yield errors of 0.023 and 0.027, compared with 0.060 and 0.110 near the depth boundaries. Together with the shared spatial structure in panel (a), these patterns motivate a shared transition predictor conditioned on source and target depth. The values are tabulated in Supplementary \Cref{tab:appendix_transition_difficulty}.

\subsection{Layer-Aware Transition Prediction}
\label{sec:predictor}

Let $A_{\theta,\ell}^t\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}$ denote the token activation produced by the diffusion transformer after block $\ell$ when processing $(x_t,t,c)$, where $N$ is the number of latent tokens and $C$ is the channel dimension. For readability, we omit $\theta$ from $A_{\theta,\ell}^t$ when the dependency is clear.

Given a source layer $a$ and target layer $b$ with $a<b$, LARA predicts the target-layer activation tokens from the source activation:
\begin{equation}
\widehat A_{a\to b}^{\,t}
=
G_\psi(A_a^t,e_a,e_b,e_t),
\qquad
\widehat A_{a\to b}^{\,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}.
\label{eq:realign_predictor}
\end{equation}
Here $G_\psi$ is a lightweight transition predictor, and $e_a$, $e_b$, $e_t$ are source-layer, target-layer, and timestep embeddings. The predicted activation $\widehat A_{a\to b}^{\,t}$ lives in the coordinate system of layer $b$. The same predictor is reused for all valid layer pairs, which removes the need to train a separate adapter for each manually selected pair.

The transition prediction loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}(\theta,\psi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{a<b,t}
\left[
d\left(
\widehat A_{a\to b}^{\,t},
\operatorname{sg}(A_b^t)
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:align_loss}
\end{equation}
where $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a tokenwise cosine distance and $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients through the target activation. In implementation, the predictor output is formed before token normalization, as detailed in \Cref{alg:lara_transition_distance}. This loss updates the predictor and the source-side backbone representation, while the target layer serves as a stable internal reference.

\subsection{Transition Consistency}

A single long transition can be difficult to learn directly (\Cref{fig:projection_layout_observation}(b,c)). To propagate supervision across distant layers without committing to one fixed pair, LARA encourages direct transitions to agree with compositions of shorter transitions. We keep an EMA copy $G_{\bar\psi}$ of the predictor for the composed target, while the intermediate $a\to b$ state is the cached online prediction from \Cref{eq:align_loss}. Thus the direct branch uses the online predictor, and the composed branch provides a stop-gradient EMA target. For $a<b<c$, we define

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat A_{a\to c}^{\,t}
=
G_\psi(\operatorname{sg}(A_a^t),e_a,e_c,e_t),
\\
\widetilde A_{a\to b\to c}^{\,t}
=
G_{\bar\psi}\!\left(
\operatorname{sg}(\widehat A_{a\to b}^{\,t}),
e_b,e_c,e_t
\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:direct_transition}
\end{equation}
Here $\bar\psi$ is updated by moving average, and the cached $\widehat A_{a\to b}^{\,t}$ is not recomputed by the EMA predictor.
The consistency loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}(\psi;\bar\psi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{a<b<c,t}
\left[
d\left(
\widehat A_{a\to c}^{\,t},
\operatorname{sg}(\widetilde A_{a\to b\to c}^{\,t})
\right)
\right].
\label{eq:consistency_loss}
\end{equation}
Only the online predictor is updated by this term. The source activation and composed target are detached, so the constraint regularizes the transition interface rather than forcing the backbone to collapse intermediate layers.



\subsection{Cross-Layer Redundancy Regularization}
LARA further introduces a cross-layer redundancy regularizer, motivated by feature decorrelation and redundancy-reduction objectives in representation learning~\citep{cogswell2016decov,huang2018decorrelated,huang2019iterative,zbontar2021barlow,bardes2022vicreg}. While compositional consistency coordinates transition mappings, multi-pair prediction also encourages information to remain recoverable across many layer pairs. This broad predictability may favor overly similar hidden states, motivating a separate constraint on representation redundancy. We therefore penalize squared cosine similarity between randomly sampled layer pairs to encourage layer-specific directions while retaining the shared structure required for cross-layer prediction. For layer pairs $(i,j)$ sampled independently of the transition pair above, we define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{div}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{i<j,t}
\left[
\operatorname{cos}^2(A_i^t,A_j^t)
\right].
\label{eq:diversity_loss}
\end{equation}
Minimizing this term discourages representation homogenization across depth. It does not enforce statistical independence between layers; it penalizes excessive directional similarity as a simple, inexpensive decorrelation signal. The ablation in \Cref{fig:cosine_squared_heatmap} examines how the strength of this penalty affects cross-layer correlation and generation quality.

\subsection{Full Objective}

The LARA regularizer assigns a separate weight to each component:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LARA}}(\theta,\psi)
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{pred}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}(\theta,\psi)
+
\\
\lambda_{\mathrm{cons}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}(\psi;\bar\psi)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{div}}(\theta).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:realign_loss}
\end{equation}
The final training objective: 
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\psi)
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LARA}}(\theta,\psi).
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
The update paths are explicit: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}$ updates the predictor and source-side backbone, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}$ updates only the predictor, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{div}}$ updates only the DiT. The training procedure and documented predictor configuration appear in Supplementary Secs.~\ref{app:realign_algorithm_compute} and \ref{app:realign_hyperparameters}; loss-weight sensitivity is reported in Supplementary \Cref{tab:main_hyperparameter_tuning}.
