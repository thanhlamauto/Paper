\section{Conclusion and Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied internal representation alignment through the predictability of transitions between diffusion-transformer layers. Transition error varies with both layer separation and source position, indicating that one fixed cross-depth relation does not describe the full hierarchy. This observation motivated LARA's shared, depth-conditioned transition predictor. A compositional consistency loss encourages agreement between direct and two-step predictions, while a cross-layer redundancy term discourages excessive directional similarity.

Under matched SiT training budgets, LARA improves ImageNet generation FID over the internal-alignment baselines across the tested model sizes. The gains extend to the tested DiT and JiT backbones, and the motion experiment reports improved HumanML3D metrics at a shorter training budget. Layerwise probes show higher scores across more of the hierarchy for the evaluated LARA checkpoint, although those checkpoints were trained for different numbers of iterations. Component ablations identify transition prediction as a useful source of cross-depth supervision and show complementary effects from the consistency and redundancy terms. Matched SiT-B/2 prediction-only controls further show that explicit depth conditioning improves FID-50K from 42.40 to 39.48, while multi-pair training improves over the tested fixed-pair $(4,7)$ predictor from 41.55 to 39.48. Because all three prediction-only variants retain a learned predictor, these results show that introducing a predictor alone does not explain the full gain: explicit depth conditioning and broader pair coverage each improve the tested setting. Together, these findings support learning cross-depth transitions as a way to use internal states for diffusion-transformer training.

\paragraph{Limitations}
LARA introduces an auxiliary transition-learning pathway during training. The shared predictor and its EMA target add trainable state and additional forward computation beyond direct pairwise alignment. In the matched SiT-XL/2 profiling setting, LARA uses a larger trainable auxiliary module than REPA (31.43M versus 8.1M parameters), although it requires less measured wall-clock time and peak memory in that comparison. Relative to the self-contained LayerSync baseline, LARA is also slower per training step (0.30\,s versus 0.25\,s). Thus, the benefit of modeling multiple cross-layer transitions comes with additional training-time complexity, and its efficiency should be understood as a trade-off rather than a uniform reduction in computational cost.

A second limitation follows from the shared-transition formulation itself. LARA assumes that cross-layer relations contain sufficient reusable structure for a common predictor to amortize transition learning across many source--target pairs, while depth conditioning accounts for pair-specific differences. Our transition analysis supports substantial shared structure, but also shows that transition difficulty changes markedly with layer separation and source position. Consequently, the quality of this approximation depends on how well a shared predictor can represent both common and depth-specific structure. Architectures whose intermediate states change more sharply in dimensionality, tokenization, or representation geometry may therefore require different projections, conditioning mechanisms, or predictor capacity. In addition, the EMA predictor used for compositional consistency should be viewed as a practical stabilization mechanism rather than a theoretically guaranteed target. The online-target ablation shows that temporal smoothing is useful in our setting, but does not establish a general convergence or stability property of the EMA construction.

Finally, LARA must balance cross-depth predictability against layer-specific diversity. Transition prediction encourages information to remain recoverable across depth, whereas the redundancy regularizer discourages different layers from becoming overly similar. These two objectives are therefore complementary but not aligned in the trivial sense that stronger decorrelation is always preferable. The redundancy-strength diagnostic shows that the setting with the lowest cross-layer correlation does not yield the best generation quality: moderate regularization gives the strongest FID and sFID, while stronger decorrelation can degrade synthesis quality. This indicates an intrinsic trade-off between preserving shared structure that makes transitions predictable and maintaining sufficient specialization across layers. Accordingly, the redundancy term should be interpreted as a mechanism for preventing excessive homogenization, rather than an objective for maximizing representation separation.
