\section{Empirical Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We study controlled SiT ImageNet generation, a system-level ImageNet comparison and direct REPA efficiency trade-off, transfer to DiT and JiT, HumanML3D motion generation, and intermediate representations. Training settings, sampling protocols, and extended sweeps are reported in Supplementary Secs.~\ref{app:experimental_details}, \ref{app:realign_ablation_protocols} and \ref{app:metric_definitions}; design controls appear in \Cref{sec:ablation_study}.

\subsection{Image Generation}
\label{sec:image_generation}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
Unless otherwise stated, we follow the class-conditional ImageNet training setup of SiT~\citep{ma2024sit}. We train on ImageNet~\citep{deng2009imagenet} at $256\times256$ resolution using the preprocessing protocol of ADM~\citep{dhariwal2021diffusion}. Each image is encoded by the Stable Diffusion VAE from LDM~\citep{rombach2022ldm} into a latent tensor $z\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times32\times4}$. We evaluate the \texttt{B/2}, \texttt{L/2}, and \texttt{XL/2} SiT configurations with patch size $2$. LARA trains the transition predictor jointly with the backbone using \Cref{eq:total_loss}. Unless specified, we use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ and batch size $256$. We sample source--target layer pairs uniformly from all valid pairs $(a,b)$ with $a<b$. Additional details on architecture, loss weights, cached-transition composition with an EMA target, training length, hardware, and compute budget are organized in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:experimental_details}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We report Frechet Inception Distance (FID)~\citep{heusel2017gans}, Inception Score (IS)~\citep{salimans2016improved,szegedy2016rethinking}, precision, and recall~\citep{kynkaanniemi2019precision} using $50\mathrm{K}$ generated samples. For the SiT, LayerSync, and LARA rows of \Cref{tab:imagenet_no_cfg}, we re-evaluate a fixed trained checkpoint with five independent sampling seeds, generating 50K images per seed. The checkpoint, sampler, sampling steps, CFG setting, class-label schedule, evaluator, and reference statistics remain fixed; we report the FID mean and sample standard deviation across those seeds. Dispersive and SRA also report FID means and sample standard deviations from sampling re-evaluations. Metric definitions are in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:metric_definitions}.

\paragraph{Comparison methods.}
Our controlled comparisons use the same SiT backbone and training budget as the base model, and compare against the self-contained internal baselines reported in \Cref{tab:imagenet_no_cfg}: Dispersive Loss~\citep{wang2025dispersive}, SRA~\citep{jiang2025sra}, and LayerSync~\citep{haghighi2026layersync}.

For system-level context in \Cref{tab:imagenet_cfg}, we include the reported ImageNet baselines that appear in the table: pixel diffusion methods ADM-U, VDM++, Simple diffusion, and CDM~\citep{dhariwal2021diffusion,kingma2023understanding,hoogeboom2023simple,ho2022cascaded}; autoregressive or predictive methods VAR and D-JEPA~\citep{tian2024visual,chen2024jepa}; latent U-Net and transformer-hybrid methods LDM, U-ViT, DiffiT, and MDTv2~\citep{rombach2022ldm,bao2023all,hatamizadeh2024diffit,gao2023mdtv2}; and transformer baselines DiT, SiT, and REPA~\citep{peebles2023scalable,ma2024sit,yu2024repa}. Detailed baseline descriptions and the reporting protocol are deferred to Supplementary Secs.~\ref{app:baseline_descriptions} and \ref{app:baseline_protocol}.

\paragraph{Results.}
ImageNet provides the main testbed because it exposes both optimization quality and scaling behavior. We compare methods within the same SiT backbone and training budget, holding model size, sampler, and guidance fixed when assessing the auxiliary alignment objectives. We then include a CFG-based system comparison to place the final model among standard ImageNet generators, where guidance can change the quality--diversity trade-off~\citep{kynkaanniemi2024guidance}.


\noindent\textbf{Training Efficiency Relative to External Alignment.}
\Cref{tab:repa_profile} complements the generation-quality comparison in \Cref{tab:imagenet_cfg} with a matched training-cost profile against REPA. Under the same SiT-XL/2 hardware, batch size, precision, data pipeline, and training budget, LARA reduces wall-clock time per step from 0.467~s to 0.300~s (35.8\%) and peak training-memory overhead from 21.97~GiB to 16.68~GiB (24.1\%). LARA also removes the 86.1M-parameter frozen representation encoder required by REPA. This efficiency is achieved by replacing external feature extraction with an internal trainable transition module: LARA uses 31.43M auxiliary trainable parameters, compared with 8.1M for REPA. Overall, the matched profile shows that LARA trades additional trainable auxiliary capacity for substantially lower measured runtime and memory while remaining self-contained.

\paragraph{Scaling across SiT model sizes.} LARA consistently improves the SiT baseline and LayerSync across the model scales and training budgets in \Cref{tab:imagenet_no_cfg}. \subsection{Cross-Architecture Transfer}
\label{sec:cross_architecture}

We test LARA on DiT-B and JiT-B using ImageNet $256\times256$, batch size 128, 300K training iterations, AdamW at learning rate $10^{-4}$, and FID-10K. As \Cref{tab:cross_architecture} shows, adding LARA reduces DiT-B FID-10K from 62.55 to 45.87, an improvement of 16.68 points (26.7\%), and JiT-B FID-10K from 48.39 to 33.71, an improvement of 14.68 points (30.3\%). The consistent direction across these two additional backbone families supports transfer of the layer-aware transition objective beyond SiT; the SiT-B/L/XL results instead test scaling within one family. These are controlled cross-architecture tests, each with one training seed and one sampling seed, rather than system-level comparisons. PixelREPA reports that external representation alignment can behave differently in pixel-space JiT models~\citep{shin2026pixelrepa}. Our JiT experiment evaluates internal cross-depth transition learning rather than regression to an external visual teacher; it does not test whether LARA resolves the failure mode identified by PixelREPA.

\subsection{Text-Conditional Human Motion Generation}
\label{sec:human_motion_generation}

To test whether LARA transfers beyond image generation, we additionally evaluate text-conditioned human motion generation on HumanML3D~\citep{guo2020action2motion,guo2022generating}. Given a text description, the model generates a sequence of human poses represented as 3D joint trajectories.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
We follow the MDM setup~\citep{tevet2022mdm} and use the same motion-transformer backbone for all rows. MDM and LayerSync are trained for $600\mathrm{K}$ iterations, while LARA is evaluated at the $300\mathrm{K}$ checkpoint reported in \Cref{tab:humanml3d_results}; the motion setup is described in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:humanml3d_details}.

We report FID and R-Precision (top 3) using the standard HumanML3D evaluation protocol. Definitions and reporting conventions are provided in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:metric_definitions}. Lower FID is better, while higher R-Precision indicates better text-motion retrieval alignment.

\textbf{Layerwise behavior.}
\Cref{fig:representation_analysis} shows higher probe scores for LARA across the hidden hierarchy in the checkpoints examined. LARA is evaluated at $500\mathrm{K}$ iterations, whereas LayerSync uses $800\mathrm{K}$ and the SiT baseline uses $2\mathrm{M}$. Averaged over layers, LARA exceeds SiT by $54.8\%$ on Tiny ImageNet classification, $113.6\%$ on PASCAL VOC segmentation, and $155.6\%$ on DINOv2-g CKA. Because these checkpoints have different training budgets, the probe comparison describes their representation profiles rather than isolating the effect of LARA at a matched iteration.

The shape of the curves adds information beyond the averages. LayerSync has a relatively sharp layerwise profile near its selected alignment region, whereas LARA's higher probe scores extend across a wider portion of the network. This pattern is consistent with LARA's multi-pair transition objective, whose supervision is distributed across source--target relations rather than concentrated on one selected pair. The broader layerwise gains support the hypothesis that transition learning distributes useful supervision across depth. The matched prediction-only control in \Cref{tab:realign_ablation} separately evaluates generation quality under multi-pair training and the fixed pair $(4,7)$; it does not establish the cause of the observed probe profiles.

\section{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation_study}

\textbf{Ablation overview.} We design the ablations around the main alternative explanations for LARA's gains. The objective could work only as generic regularization, depend on delicate loss weights, or require an oversized predictor. Each control changes one factor while keeping the backbone, training budget, and evaluation protocol fixed whenever possible. Extended controls are reported in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:realign_ablation_protocols}.

\subsection{Objective Decomposition}
\label{sec:objective_decomposition}

\textbf{Prediction as the core signal.} \Cref{tab:realign_ablation} first isolates transition prediction from the two auxiliary regularizers. With explicit depth conditioning and multi-pair training, prediction alone reduces FID-50K from 47.51 for the vanilla model to 39.48, also improving over LayerSync at 41.89. Learning cross-depth transitions thus provides a strong training signal before adding consistency or redundancy regularization.

\textbf{Depth conditioning and multi-pair supervision.} We next isolate the two design choices behind the shared transition predictor. Removing explicit depth conditioning while retaining multi-pair prediction increases FID-50K from 39.48 to 42.40, showing that source and target layer identities provide useful information beyond the source activation and timestep. Conversely, retaining depth conditioning but restricting prediction to the fixed pair $(4,7)$ gives 41.55 FID-50K, compared with 39.48 when the same predictor is trained across multiple layer pairs. These matched controls show that introducing an auxiliary predictor alone does not explain the full gain: relation-specific conditioning and broader cross-depth supervision each contribute in the tested setting. The fixed-pair result concerns the tested pair $(4,7)$, rather than every possible pair.

\textbf{Complementary regularization of the transition family.} Multi-pair prediction improves over the tested fixed relation, but it also exposes the shared predictor to transitions of different difficulty and encourages information to remain recoverable across a broader portion of the hierarchy. Starting from 39.48 FID-50K, compositional consistency reduces FID to 38.07, while redundancy regularization yields a larger isolated reduction to 36.14. Combining both objectives further improves FID to 34.43. By comparison, redundancy regularization alone reaches 43.02. These results support transition prediction as the primary cross-depth supervision, with consistency and redundancy regularization strengthening the resulting multi-pair transition family.

\textbf{Interpreting the auxiliary objectives.} The diagnostics provide a consistent explanation for these gains. Transition error rises from 0.025 in the shortest-gap quartile to 0.152 in the longest (\Cref{fig:projection_layout_observation}(b)), suggesting that shorter transitions offer useful intermediate structure for learning more difficult long-range mappings. This motivates compositional consistency, which encourages a direct long-range prediction to agree with a composition of shorter transitions. At the same time, broad cross-depth predictability creates a tension between information sharing and layer specialization. The redundancy-strength sweep in \Cref{fig:cosine_squared_heatmap} shows that moderate decorrelation improves generation quality, whereas stronger decorrelation does not, supporting redundancy reduction as a balancing constraint rather than an objective for maximizing layer separation. Together, these results motivate a division of roles: depth conditioning specializes the mapping to each relation, multi-pair prediction broadens supervision, consistency structures long-range transition learning, and redundancy regularization preserves layer-specific variation.

\textbf{Consistency target.} We next examine how the compositional target should evolve during training. In the matched FID-10K ablation in \Cref{tab:ema_consistency_target}, removing consistency gives 36.91, while using a stop-gradient copy of the online predictor slightly worsens FID to 37.20. Replacing this target with the slowly updated EMA predictor improves FID to 35.12. The benefit therefore comes not simply from adding a composition constraint, but from providing that constraint with a more slowly evolving target. We use the EMA predictor for compositional consistency as a practical stabilization mechanism.

\subsection{Functional Layer-Aware Transition Diagnostic}
\label{sec:inference_transition_diagnostic}

\textbf{Functional transition diagnostic.} We further test whether the learned predictor produces target-depth states that are functionally compatible with the downstream backbone. For the transition from block 3 to block 7, we bypass the intervening transformer blocks and resume computation either directly from the unchanged block-3 state or from the predictor-generated state for block 7. This intervention isolates the contribution of the learned cross-depth transition while keeping the skipped interval fixed.

The difference is substantial. Naively bypassing the block-3-to-block-7 interval without the predictor degrades FID to 128.25. Replacing the same skipped computation with the predictor-generated target-depth state reduces FID to 54.17 while retaining the same relative block cost of $0.67\times$. Restricting the predictor transition to the first 125 of 250 denoising steps further improves FID to 45.84, approaching the full-backbone result of 35.12 while executing fewer transformer blocks overall.

The key comparison is therefore between bypassing the same depth interval with and without the learned transition. The reduction from 128.25 to 54.17 FID shows that the predictor supplies substantial information needed by the downstream layers, rather than merely benefiting from a different inference schedule. This provides functional evidence that LARA learns a target-depth representation that can be consumed by the remaining backbone. The standard generation results nevertheless use the full backbone; this intervention is intended as a diagnostic of the learned transition interface.
