\section{Empirical Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We study controlled SiT ImageNet generation, a system-level ImageNet comparison and direct REPA efficiency trade-off, transfer to DiT and JiT, HumanML3D motion generation, and intermediate representations. Training settings, sampling protocols, and extended sweeps are reported in Supplementary Secs.~\ref{app:experimental_details}, \ref{app:realign_ablation_protocols} and \ref{app:metric_definitions}; design controls appear in \Cref{sec:ablation_study}.

\subsection{Image Generation}
\label{sec:image_generation}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
Unless otherwise stated, we follow the class-conditional ImageNet training setup of SiT~\citep{ma2024sit}. We train on ImageNet~\citep{deng2009imagenet} at $256\times256$ resolution using the preprocessing protocol of ADM~\citep{dhariwal2021diffusion}. Each image is encoded by the Stable Diffusion VAE from LDM~\citep{rombach2022ldm} into a latent tensor $z\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times32\times4}$. We evaluate the \texttt{B/2}, \texttt{L/2}, and \texttt{XL/2} SiT configurations with patch size $2$. LARA trains the transition predictor jointly with the backbone using \Cref{eq:total_loss}. Unless specified, we use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ and batch size $256$. We sample source--target layer pairs uniformly from all valid pairs $(a,b)$ with $a<b$. Additional details on architecture, loss weights, cached-transition composition with an EMA target, training length, hardware, and compute budget are organized in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:experimental_details}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We report Frechet Inception Distance (FID)~\citep{heusel2017gans}, Inception Score (IS)~\citep{salimans2016improved,szegedy2016rethinking}, precision, and recall~\citep{kynkaanniemi2019precision} using $50\mathrm{K}$ generated samples. For the SiT, LayerSync, and LARA rows of \Cref{tab:imagenet_no_cfg}, we re-evaluate a fixed trained checkpoint with five independent sampling seeds, generating 50K images per seed. The checkpoint, sampler, sampling steps, CFG setting, class-label schedule, evaluator, and reference statistics remain fixed; we report the FID mean and sample standard deviation across those seeds. Dispersive and SRA also report FID means and sample standard deviations from sampling re-evaluations. Metric definitions are in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:metric_definitions}.

\paragraph{Comparison methods.}
Our controlled comparisons use the same SiT backbone and training budget as the base model, and compare against the self-contained internal baselines reported in \Cref{tab:imagenet_no_cfg}: Dispersive Loss~\citep{wang2025dispersive}, SRA~\citep{jiang2025sra}, and LayerSync~\citep{haghighi2026layersync}.

For system-level context in \Cref{tab:imagenet_cfg}, we include the reported ImageNet baselines that appear in the table: pixel diffusion methods ADM-U, VDM++, Simple diffusion, and CDM~\citep{dhariwal2021diffusion,kingma2023understanding,hoogeboom2023simple,ho2022cascaded}; autoregressive or predictive methods VAR and D-JEPA~\citep{tian2024visual,chen2024jepa}; latent U-Net and transformer-hybrid methods LDM, U-ViT, DiffiT, and MDTv2~\citep{rombach2022ldm,bao2023all,hatamizadeh2024diffit,gao2023mdtv2}; and transformer baselines DiT, SiT, and REPA~\citep{peebles2023scalable,ma2024sit,yu2024repa}. Detailed baseline descriptions and the reporting protocol are deferred to Supplementary Secs.~\ref{app:baseline_descriptions} and \ref{app:baseline_protocol}.

\paragraph{Results.}
ImageNet provides the main testbed because it exposes both optimization quality and scaling behavior. We compare methods within the same SiT backbone and training budget, holding model size, sampler, and guidance fixed when assessing the auxiliary alignment objectives. We then include a CFG-based system comparison to place the final model among standard ImageNet generators, where guidance can change the quality--diversity trade-off~\citep{kynkaanniemi2024guidance}.


\paragraph{Quality--Efficiency Trade-off with External Alignment.}
\Cref{tab:repa_profile} exposes a different trade-off from the generation-quality comparison in \Cref{tab:imagenet_cfg}. Under the matched SiT-XL/2 profiling setup, LARA reduces per-step training time from 0.467~s to 0.300~s (35.8\%) and peak training-memory overhead from 21.97~GiB to 16.68~GiB (24.1\%) relative to REPA. It also removes the 86.1M frozen parameters of the external representation encoder. These savings come with a larger trainable auxiliary module: 31.43M parameters for LARA versus 8.1M for REPA. Thus, LARA does not uniformly reduce model complexity; it shifts training cost from an external encoder to an internal trainable transition module, yielding lower measured runtime and memory in this setup. The system-level FID values in \Cref{tab:imagenet_cfg} are not obtained from these profiling runs.

\paragraph{Scaling across SiT model sizes.} LARA consistently improves the SiT baseline and LayerSync across the model scales and training budgets in \Cref{tab:imagenet_no_cfg}. At 400K iterations, it reduces FID relative to LayerSync by 3.57, 1.97, and 2.50 points on SiT-B/2, SiT-L/2, and SiT-XL/2, respectively. The advantage persists at longer budgets, with improvements of 1.59 FID at 1M and 1.73 FID at 2M on SiT-XL/2. These gaps exceed the observed fixed-checkpoint sampling variability: across the re-evaluated settings, the sampling-seed standard deviation ranges from 0.02 to 0.13. Thus, the gains are not explained by a favorable sampling seed, although these error bars do not measure variability across independently trained models. In the separate CFG system comparison of \Cref{tab:imagenet_cfg}, LARA records 1.80 FID versus 1.93 for LayerSync.

\subsection{Cross-Architecture Transfer}
\label{sec:cross_architecture}

We test LARA on DiT-B and JiT-B using ImageNet $256\times256$, batch size 128, 300K training iterations, AdamW at learning rate $10^{-4}$, and FID-10K. As \Cref{tab:cross_architecture} shows, adding LARA reduces DiT-B FID-10K from 62.55 to 45.87, an improvement of 16.68 points (26.7\%), and JiT-B FID-10K from 48.39 to 33.71, an improvement of 14.68 points (30.3\%). The consistent direction across these two additional backbone families supports transfer of the layer-aware transition objective beyond SiT; the SiT-B/L/XL results instead test scaling within one family. These are controlled cross-architecture tests, each with one training seed and one sampling seed, rather than system-level comparisons.

\subsection{Text-Conditional Human Motion Generation}
\label{sec:human_motion_generation}

To test whether LARA transfers beyond image generation, we additionally evaluate text-conditioned human motion generation on HumanML3D~\citep{guo2020action2motion,guo2022generating}. Given a text description, the model generates a sequence of human poses represented as 3D joint trajectories.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
We follow the MDM setup~\citep{tevet2022mdm} and use the same motion-transformer backbone for all rows. MDM and LayerSync are trained for $600\mathrm{K}$ iterations, while LARA is evaluated at the $300\mathrm{K}$ checkpoint reported in \Cref{tab:humanml3d_results}; the motion setup is described in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:humanml3d_details}.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
We report FID and R-Precision (top 3) using the standard HumanML3D evaluation protocol. Definitions and reporting conventions are provided in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:metric_definitions}. Lower FID is better, while higher R-Precision indicates better text-motion retrieval alignment.

The probes test complementary properties: Tiny ImageNet classification measures semantic exposure, PASCAL VOC segmentation measures spatial localization, and DINOv2 CKA measures alignment with strong visual features. \textbf{Layerwise behavior.}
\Cref{fig:representation_analysis} shows higher probe scores for LARA across the hidden hierarchy in the checkpoints examined. LARA is evaluated at $500\mathrm{K}$ iterations, whereas LayerSync uses $800\mathrm{K}$ and the SiT baseline uses $2\mathrm{M}$. Averaged over layers, LARA exceeds SiT by $54.8\%$ on Tiny ImageNet classification, $113.6\%$ on PASCAL VOC segmentation, and $155.6\%$ on DINOv2-g CKA. It also exceeds LayerSync by $20.9\%$, $21.6\%$, and $9.5\%$, respectively. The best layer reaches $55.71$ classification accuracy, $47.15$ segmentation mIoU, and $0.287$ CKA. Because these checkpoints have different training budgets, the probe comparison describes their representation profiles rather than isolating the effect of LARA at a matched iteration.

The shape of the curves adds information beyond the averages. LayerSync has a relatively sharp layerwise profile near its selected alignment region, whereas LARA's higher probe scores extend across a wider portion of the network. This pattern is consistent with LARA's multi-pair transition objective, whose supervision is distributed across source--target relations rather than concentrated on one selected pair. The broader layerwise gains support the hypothesis that transition learning distributes useful supervision across depth; isolating the contribution of multi-pair training would require a matched single-pair predictor control.

\section{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation_study}

\textbf{Ablation overview.} We design the ablations around the main alternative explanations for LARA's gains. The objective could work only as generic regularization, depend on delicate loss weights, or require an oversized predictor. Each control changes one factor while keeping the backbone, training budget, and evaluation protocol fixed whenever possible. Extended controls are reported in Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:realign_ablation_protocols}.

\label{sec:objective_decomposition}

\textbf{Objective decomposition.} \Cref{tab:realign_ablation} uses FID-50K to separate transition prediction from its auxiliary regularizers. Prediction alone improves FID from 47.51 for the vanilla model to 41.48, slightly surpassing LayerSync at 41.89. The cross-layer redundancy term alone reaches 43.02, so decorrelation by itself does not account for the full gain. When added to prediction, this term gives the larger isolated improvement in this setting, reducing FID from 41.48 to 36.14, whereas prediction with consistency reaches 38.07. The regularizers are nevertheless complementary: adding consistency to prediction plus the redundancy term further reduces FID to 34.43. These results support a decomposition in which prediction supplies cross-depth supervision, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{div}}$ discourages excessive directional similarity between layers, and consistency constrains composed transitions. Consistency alone is omitted because its transition paths require prediction to anchor them to target-layer representations.

\textbf{Consistency target.} To isolate the role of the composed target, \Cref{tab:ema_consistency_target} compares no transition consistency, a stop-gradient online target, and the EMA target used by LARA under the same FID-10K ablation protocol. The online target slightly worsens FID relative to omitting consistency (37.20 versus 36.91), whereas the slowly updated EMA target improves it to 35.12. EMA is therefore a practical stabilization mechanism in this setting: the compositional constraint helps when its target evolves more slowly than the online predictor.

\textbf{Redundancy-strength trade-off.} \Cref{fig:cosine_squared_heatmap} examines the effect of the diversity weight in a separate 100K-step SiT-B diagnostic. Without the penalty, mean off-diagonal correlation is 0.82353 and FID-10K is 78.63. At $2\times$, correlation falls to 0.66316, FID improves to 66.68, and sFID improves from 137.21 to 124.66. Increasing the weight to $4\times$ lowers correlation further to 0.62781, but FID worsens to 76.76 and sFID to 144.38. At $8\times$, correlation rebounds to 0.76206 without a quality gain. Thus, the lowest correlation and the best generation quality occur at different tested weights.

\textbf{Interaction with transition prediction.} The objective decomposition in \Cref{tab:realign_ablation} gives a complementary control: adding diversity regularization to prediction reduces FID-50K from 41.48 to 36.14, while diversity alone reaches 43.02. This supports using the two objectives together in that setting. Prediction encourages target-relevant information to remain recoverable from source-layer states, while the diversity penalty discourages excessive directional similarity across layers. The sweep is consistent with a balance between these objectives: moderate regularization reduces redundancy while retaining useful cross-depth structure, whereas stronger decorrelation need not improve generation. This interpretation does not require statistical independence between layers; nor does the observed correlation alone establish the mechanism behind the FID change.

\textbf{Loss-weight and timestep sensitivity.} \Cref{tab:main_sensitivity_summary} reports the matched SiT-B/2 FID-10K sweep, varying one factor at a time around the selected recipe. Across the tested alternatives, FID remains within 0.76 of the selected setting's 35.12. These local ranges suggest that the recipe does not depend on a sharply tuned loss weight, while the selected combination remains best among the tested values.

\subsection{Predictor Design and Capacity}
\label{sec:predictor_ablation}

\Cref{tab:predictor_architecture_ablation,tab:main_predictor_size_ablation} test whether LARA's gains depend on a particular predictor design or simply on added auxiliary capacity. In the architecture comparison, parameter counts are tightly controlled around 13M, and all variants use the same timestep, class, source-layer, target-layer, and gap conditioning with FiLM/AdaLN-style scale and shift. CNN, MLP, ConvNeXt + Attention, and ViT predictors yield FID-10K between 35.12 and 36.65. ConvNeXt + Attention performs best, but the 1.53-FID spread across different designs indicates that the transition objective is not tied to one predictor family; in particular, the MLP remains effective without the default spatial ConvNeXt design.

\subsection{Inference-Time Transition Diagnostic}
\label{sec:inference_transition_diagnostic}

The predictor is trained to align intermediate states, so we also test whether its output can replace four of twelve backbone blocks at inference. \Cref{tab:main_layer_skip_diagnostic} reports the controlled diagnostic from Supplementary Sec.~\ref{app:magnitude_layer_skip}. The direction-only route substantially worsens FID. A detached magnitude head recovers part of the loss, especially when skipping is limited to the first 125 of 250 Euler steps, but it remains worse than the full backbone. Thus, the primary generation results use the full sampler; learning a useful alignment target does not by itself make the predictor an accurate block substitute.
